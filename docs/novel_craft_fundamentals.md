# Novel Craft Fundamentals: What Editors, Agents, Teachers and Craft Books Agree On

Scope: genre-agnostic novel craft. Each section is organized so it can be lifted into a checklist or a specialist reviewer "skill": what the component is, why it matters, criteria/tests, failure modes, and named sources.

## 1. Structure: frameworks and the beats they share

### Takeaway
Every mainstream framework (three-act, Save the Cat, Weiland, Seven-Point, Story Grid, Hero's Journey) converges on the same skeleton: a 25/50/25 split with an inciting disruption early in Act 1, a point-of-no-return commitment at ~20-25%, a midpoint reversal at ~50%, a lowest point at ~75%, and a climax at ~85-90%. Kishōtenketsu and Jane Alison's shapes are the notable non-conflict/non-arc alternatives and should be flagged as such rather than forced into the arc template.

### Cited Findings

**Three-act proportions and the "seven key plot points"**
- Three-act structure allocates Act One 25%, Act Two 50%, Act Three 25% (novel or film). The seven key plot points: Hook (first few pages), Inciting Incident (~6-15%), First Plot Point (25%), Midpoint/Second Plot Point (50%), Third Plot Point (75%), Climax (middle of Act 3), Resolution (second half of Act 3) — [Aliventures, Three Act Story Structure](https://www.aliventures.com/three-act-structure/)
- Same 25/50/25 split summarized as: Act 1 setup + inciting incident; Act 2 confrontation and complications; Act 3 resolution — [BMAD story-structures reference](https://git.biohazardvfx.com/Nicholai/BMAD-METHOD/raw/branch/ai-agent-system/expansion-packs/bmad-creative-writing/data/story-structures.md)

**K.M. Weiland (Structuring Your Novel) beat percentages**
- Hook (near beginning); Inciting Event ~12% (introduces main conflict); Key Event (protagonist's response, no % given); First Plot Point ~25% ("Doorway of No Return", protagonist commits fully); First Pinch Point ~37% (antagonist's force pressures protagonist); Midpoint/Second Plot Point ~50% (internal and external conflict converge; new information causes paradigm shift); Second Pinch Point ~62% (protagonist realizes what is at stake); Third Plot Point ~75% ("dark night of the soul", a cherished lie dies, protagonist must decide whether to keep fighting); Climax ~88%; Resolution (loose ends). Percentages described as approximate — [Janet's Writing Blog summarizing Weiland](https://janetswritingblog.com/2022/01/17/trying-to-get-novel-structure-right/)
- Pinch points: First Pinch Point ~37.5% (midway between First Plot Point and Midpoint) — reader must *see or experience* the antagonist, not just hear about it; cutaway scene acceptable. Second Pinch Point ~62.5% needs an entire scene showing the antagonist's "nature, power, and essence." Pinch points maintain rising action, show the opposition and stakes, set up the hero's emotional change. "The simpler and more direct the pinch point the better." Use at least two — [Writers Helping Writers, What Are Pinch Points](https://writershelpingwriters.net/2021/10/what-are-pinch-points-and-where-do-they-go/)

**Save the Cat (Blake Snyder; Jessica Brody's novel adaptation)**
- 15 beats with % positions: Opening Image (0-1%); Theme Stated (5%); Setup (1-10%); Catalyst (10%); Debate (10-20%); Break Into Two (20%); B Story (22%); Fun and Games (20-50%); Midpoint (50%, false victory or false defeat, stakes rise); Bad Guys Close In (50-75%); All Is Lost (75%); Dark Night of the Soul (75-80%); Break Into Three (80%, hero realizes key truth); Finale (80-99%); Final Image (99-100%, mirrors/contrasts opening image) — [Reedsy Save the Cat beat sheet](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/save-the-cat-beat-sheet/) and [BMAD story-structures](https://git.biohazardvfx.com/Nicholai/BMAD-METHOD/raw/branch/ai-agent-system/expansion-packs/bmad-creative-writing/data/story-structures.md). Note: Reedsy lists Break Into Two as "Page 25 / 30%", which looks like a typo versus the screenplay page 25 of 110 (~23%) and BMAD's 20%; treat Break Into Two as ~20-25%.
- Snyder's original screenplay page markers (110-page script): Opening Image p1, Theme Stated p5, Set-up pp1-10, Catalyst p12, Debate pp12-25, Break into Two p25, B Story p30, Fun and Games pp30-55, Midpoint p55, Bad Guys Close In pp55-75, All Is Lost p75, Dark Night pp75-85, Break into Three p85, Finale pp85-110, Final Image p110. Acts labeled Thesis / Antithesis / Synthesis — [E.A. Deverell Plot Formula Cheatsheet](https://www.eadeverell.com/wp-content/uploads/EA-Deverell-Plot-Formula-Cheatsheet.pdf)

**Seven-Point Structure (Dan Wells)**
- Hook (starting state, the *opposite* of the Resolution); Plot Turn 1 (call to adventure, kicks off story); Pinch 1 (pressure forces characters into action; villain introduced); Midpoint (shift from reacting to acting); Pinch 2 (pressure peaks, plan fails, often loss of mentor, villain seems to win); Plot Turn 2 (last piece of information needed to win); Resolution (characters act, showing how they changed). Wells advises planning the Resolution first and working backward to the Hook so the character has a real arc — [Writing Excuses 7.41](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-7-41-seven-point-story-structure/); [Deverell cheatsheet](https://www.eadeverell.com/wp-content/uploads/EA-Deverell-Plot-Formula-Cheatsheet.pdf)

**Story Grid (Shawn Coyne)**
- Global story = Beginning Hook / Middle Build / Ending Payoff at roughly 25/50/25. For a 100,000-word novel: ~25k / 50k / 25k words. Recommends 50-60 scenes of ~2,000 words (contemporary commercial fiction scenes run 1,000-5,000 words); minimum 15 scenes (five per section); remaining scenes split by the 25/50/25 ratio. Explicitly a planning guide, not a rule — [Story Grid, How Many Scenes in a Novel](https://storygrid.com/414/)
- Beginning Hook must make readers curious enough not to abandon the story, raise questions the story promises to answer, and present a choice with no good options. Middle Build escalates via progressive complications forcing high-stakes risks. Ending Payoff fulfills the hook's promises "in completely unique and unexpected ways," is surprising yet inevitable, answers the central question, restores order — [Story Grid, Hook Build Payoff](https://storygrid.com/hook-build-payoff/)
- Across the three sections Coyne suggests an irreversible action by the end of the Beginning Hook, a point of no return in the Middle Build, another irreversible action at the end of the Middle Build, and an irreversible character change by the end of the Ending Payoff — [Story Grid, 5 Commandments Revisited](https://storygrid.com/5-commandments-storytelling-revisited/)
- Editor's Six Core Questions: (1) Genre (the promise to the reader; pick one and deliver fully); (2) Conventions and obligatory scenes (find them by analyzing masterworks); (3) Point of view (choose and keep consistent; unexplained shifts signal weak craft); (4) Objects of desire; (5) Controlling idea/theme; (6) Beginning hook / middle build / ending payoff — [Story Grid, Editor's Six Core Questions](https://storygrid.com/editors-six-core-questions-part-1/)

**Hero's Journey (Campbell / Vogler)**
- Vogler's 12 stages: Ordinary World; Call to Adventure; Refusal; Meeting the Mentor; Crossing the Threshold; Tests, Allies, Enemies; Approach to the Inmost Cave; Ordeal; Reward; Road Back; Resurrection; Return with the Elixir — [BMAD story-structures](https://git.biohazardvfx.com/Nicholai/BMAD-METHOD/raw/branch/ai-agent-system/expansion-packs/bmad-creative-writing/data/story-structures.md)
- Mapping to acts (DIY MFA): Act I = Ordinary World (hook) through Crossing the First Threshold (= First Plot Point); Act II = Tests/Allies/Enemies (midpoint region), Approach, Ordeal (= Third Plot Point), Reward; Act III = Road Back, Resurrection (= Climax), Return with Elixir (= climactic moment). Refusal, Mentor, Approach, Road Back marked optional — [DIY MFA, Deep Dive Into the Hero's Journey](https://diymfa.com/writing/a-deep-dive-into-the-heros-journey)
- Campbell's original 17 stages in Separation / Initiation / Return — [Deverell cheatsheet](https://www.eadeverell.com/wp-content/uploads/EA-Deverell-Plot-Formula-Cheatsheet.pdf)

**Freytag's Pyramid**
- Exposition, Rising Action, Climax, Falling Action, Denouement (no percentages) — [BMAD story-structures](https://git.biohazardvfx.com/Nicholai/BMAD-METHOD/raw/branch/ai-agent-system/expansion-packs/bmad-creative-writing/data/story-structures.md)

**Kishōtenketsu (four-part, non-conflict)**
- Ki (introduction: grounded, familiar world); Shō (development: deepen without sudden plot turns, "rhythm, not revolution"); Ten (twist: reframes earlier parts, shifts perspective rather than escalating stakes or adding a villain); Ketsu (conclusion: balance, "harmony restored"). Requires no central conflict; tension comes from wondering how elements connect; payoff from understanding rather than winning — [StorytellingDB, Kishōtenketsu](https://storytellingdb.com/kishotenketsu/)

**Jane Alison, Meander Spiral Explode**
- Argues against the Aristotelian arc that "swells and tautens until climax, then collapses" as the only shape. Names alternatives: waves, meanders (winding, kinking paths), spirals (common in reflective first-person novels), radials/explosions (narratives radiating from a central incident, pain or absence), cells, networks, fractals. Reviewer notes her "literature is stuck on Aristotle" thesis is "a bit of a straw man" — [Riot Material review](https://www.riotmaterial.com/meander-spiral-explode-design-and-pattern-in-narrative/)

**Other structural principles**
- Sanderson: every story should answer one major dramatic question, clear by the end of Act One; stakes need gradual buildup (sudden jumps feel unearned); a midpoint shift should pivot the story; structure is a diagnostic tool, not a dictator ("be a chef, not a cook"); "hang a lantern" on plot oddities sparingly — [Brandon Sanderson 2025 Lecture 3](https://brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-2025-overview-of-story-structure-lecture-3)
- Try/fail cycles: the character faces several failures, each harder than the last, before success (example: two failures then success). Failures should arise naturally and reveal the character's limits, the villain's strength, or knowledge gaps. "Yes, but" = solves the problem but with complications; "No, and" = fails and things get worse; each step escalates — [Sanderson Lecture 3](https://brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-2025-overview-of-story-structure-lecture-3)

### Inferences
- Consensus beat map (merge of Weiland, Snyder, Wells, Coyne, Vogler): Hook 0-1% → Inciting Incident ~10-12% → First Plot Point / Break Into Two / Crossing Threshold ~20-25% → Pinch 1 ~37% → Midpoint reversal ~50% (reactive→proactive; false victory/defeat) → Pinch 2 ~62% → Third Plot Point / All Is Lost / Ordeal ~75% → Dark Night 75-80% → Break Into Three ~80% → Climax ~85-90% → Resolution/Final Image 95-100%.
- Reviewer checklist derived from the above: (a) Is there a disruption by ~12%? (b) Is there an irreversible commitment by ~25%? (c) Does the antagonist's force get *shown* on page near 37% and 62%? (d) Does something at 50% change the protagonist's understanding and flip them from reacting to acting? (e) Is there a genuine low at ~75% where the old belief/plan dies? (f) Does the climax decide the story question the hook posed? (g) Does the ending mirror/contrast the opening state (Wells's Hook-is-opposite-of-Resolution; Snyder's Opening/Final Image)?
- Kishōtenketsu and Alison's shapes should be treated as deliberate alternatives; a reviewer should ask which model the author is using before diagnosing "missing conflict."

### Gaps
- Hero's Journey and Freytag have no canonical percentage markers in any source found; percentages shown are inferred from the act mapping.
- Did not find a primary-source statement of Weiland's "Key Event" percentage.

## 2. Scene craft

### Takeaway
The two dominant scene models (Swain/Bickham's Scene-Sequel and Coyne's Five Commandments + value shift) agree that a scene earns its place only if a character pursues something, meets opposition, and ends in a different state than it began; "in late, out early" governs the edges.

### Cited Findings
- Swain's Scene = Goal, Conflict, Setback (Ingermanson's "GCS Scene"); Sequel = Reaction, Dilemma, Decision ("RDD Scene"). Scenes and sequels typically run 1-12 pages, average ~4; Ingermanson fills ~10-12 page chapters with them and says "Chapters really aren't that important" — [Advanced Fiction Writing, Scenes, Sequels and Chapters](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/blog/2009/05/25/scenes-sequels-and-chapters/)
- Story Grid Five Commandments at scene level: (1) Inciting Incident, causal (another person) or coincidental (an event); (2) Progressive Complications, each harder than the last and never repeating the same level, culminating in a Turning Point (the final complication that shifts the scene from safe to dangerous); (3) Crisis, a question posed by the turning point, either Best Bad Choice or Irreconcilable Goods; (4) Climax, the action the character takes ("actions reveal character more than words"); (5) Resolution, outcome and effect, either resolving or becoming the next scene's inciting incident. All five should appear in every scene, even subtly — [Story Grid, 5 Commandments Revisited](https://storygrid.com/5-commandments-storytelling-revisited/)
- Value shift test: a scene must shift a value and needs a turning point; otherwise "the passage is exposition with nothing happening." Identify the protagonist's value at start and end and state it "from X to Y" (e.g., safe to unsafe). Core scenes must shift the global value; others may shift a subplot value. If a scene starts and ends negative, the two negative charges must differ in weight. The turning point is the last progressive complication, where the protagonist must change tactics, literally (new strategy) or psychologically (new awareness). Master single-turning-point scenes first — [Story Grid, Value Shift 101](https://storygrid.com/value-shift-101/)
- Values should alternate polarity rather than stack in one direction (negative → double negative → triple negative feels unrealistic) — [Story Grid, 5 Commandments Revisited](https://storygrid.com/5-commandments-storytelling-revisited/)
- "In late, out early" (attributed to William Goldman, also Mamet): each scene contains only what the story needs; no arrivals, departures, greetings, small talk. To find the latest entry, cut material from the beginning and check if the scene still works. Leave as soon as the scene's goal is achieved; unless final, end with a question unanswered. Exceptions: a slower scene can give a breather or rebuild tension at the start of an act, but must be made interesting — [The Story Department, In Late, Out Early](https://www.thestorydepartment.com/screenwriting-in-late-out-early/)
- Lisa Cron's cause-and-effect scene matrix: for each scene ask what external event occurs, how the protagonist interprets it through the misbelief, what they do, and what consequences set up the next scene. "Every action must have a reaction"; the protagonist should be different at the end of each scene — [StorytellingDB Story Genius](https://storytellingdb.com/story-genius-method-lisa-cron/); [Shortform, Wired for Story](https://www.shortform.com/pdf/wired-for-story-pdf-lisa-cron)
- Developmental editor's scene pass: check that each scene *turns* and that dialogue does more than deliver information — [Editor World, How to Revise a Novel](https://www.editorworld.com/article/how-to-revise-a-novel)
- Egri's conflict taxonomy: Static (characters can't decide, drama stalls); Jumping (transitional steps between emotional states are skipped, shift feels unearned); Rising (the natural result of a clear premise and uncompromising characters) — [SuperSummary, Art of Dramatic Writing](https://www.supersummary.com/the-art-of-dramatic-writing/summary)

### Inferences
- A scene-earns-its-place test combining the sources: (1) Name the POV character's goal in one line. (2) Name the opposition. (3) State the value shift "from __ to __" with polarity. (4) Point to the sentence where the turning point happens and the character changes tactics. (5) Name the crisis choice (best bad / irreconcilable goods). (6) Could the first paragraph be cut? The last? (7) What does the next scene inherit from this one? If (3) or (4) cannot be answered, the passage is exposition or a sequel masquerading as a scene.
- Sequels (reaction/dilemma/decision) are legitimate and necessary for pacing, but a reviewer should flag any run of two or more consecutive sequels without a goal-driven scene.

### Gaps
- No source gave a hard ratio of scenes to sequels; Ingermanson's only guidance is page length.

## 3. Character

### Takeaway
Across Weiland, Truby, Cron, Egri and Story Grid, a well-built protagonist has a conscious Want, an unconscious Need, a Lie/misbelief rooted in a wound/ghost, and an antagonist who competes for the same goal and embodies the path the protagonist could take; arcs are positive, flat, or negative (disillusionment, fall, corruption).

### Cited Findings
- Weiland: the Lie is a false belief rooted in past experience that blocks growth; the Truth is what the character learns (positive) or already holds (flat); Want = conscious external pursuit, Need = deeper inner change; Ghost = the past wound that shaped the Lie. Three arcs: Positive Change (shed Lie, embrace Truth), Flat (already holds Truth, changes the world around them), Negative Change with subtypes Disillusionment, Descent/Fall, Moral degradation/Corruption. Arc beats: opening under the Lie; First Plot Point (~25%) challenges the Lie; Midpoint moment of clarity separates Lie from Truth; late Act 2 possible "misleading victory" where want and need seem compatible; Third Plot Point (~75%) forces choice between Lie and Truth; Climax tests commitment to Truth — [Shortform, Creating Character Arcs](https://www.shortform.com/pdf/creating-character-arcs-pdf-k-m-weiland)
- Truby's seven steps: Weakness and need; Desire; Opponent; Plan; Battle; Self-revelation; New equilibrium. "Give your hero a moral need as well as a psychological need" (moral need widens the character's effect on others and keeps them from being perfect or a victim). The true opponent competes with the hero for the same goal, forcing repeated direct conflict; find them by starting from the hero's specific goal and asking who wants to stop it. Designing principle = story process + original execution — [Goodreads notes on Anatomy of Story](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/213909983-the-anatomy-of-story/47131435-robin-reed)
- Truby's 22 steps include Ghost and story world, fake-ally opponent, opponent's main counterattack, attack by ally, apparent defeat, three revelation-and-decision beats, audience revelation, gate/gauntlet/visit to death, battle, self-revelation, moral decision, new equilibrium — [Deverell cheatsheet](https://www.eadeverell.com/wp-content/uploads/EA-Deverell-Plot-Formula-Cheatsheet.pdf)
- Cron (Story Genius): the misbelief is a childhood-formed worldview creating the gap between surface want and need; the "third rail" is the connection between internal struggle and external plot, and every scene should advance both; origin scene (~2,000 words, first person) shows the healthy belief the misbelief replaced and how the character made meaning of the loss; three reinforcing "crossroads" moments; plot-testing questions: Does the problem confront the misbelief? Create a real dilemma? Matter emotionally? Support several escalating scenes? — [StorytellingDB Story Genius](https://storytellingdb.com/story-genius-method-lisa-cron/)
- Cron (Wired for Story): "great stories are about how people change"; the internal goal is what makes external goals hard; something must contradict a belief the protagonist holds; don't protect the protagonist — [Shortform, Wired for Story](https://www.shortform.com/pdf/wired-for-story-pdf-lisa-cron)
- Egri: three dimensions of character (physiological, sociological, psychological); orchestration = deliberately contrasting character types; unity of opposites = a bond so strong neither side can compromise without destroying the conflict; the antagonist must be equal in strength to the protagonist; pivotal character is the driving force — [SuperSummary, Art of Dramatic Writing](https://www.supersummary.com/the-art-of-dramatic-writing/summary)
- Story Grid on antagonists: the antagonist mirrors the protagonist, faced a comparable choice earlier and chose the opposite; "represents the path your Protagonist could take—but must resist"; need not be a villain (Buzz in Toy Story "gets in the way of what the Protagonist wants and forces them to change"). Prescriptive stories reward the right choice; Cautionary stories punish the wrong one — [Story Grid, Protagonist and Antagonist](https://storygrid.com/protagonist-and-antagonist/)
- Seven-Point: Hook state should be the opposite of the Resolution state so there is a real arc — [Writing Excuses 7.41](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-7-41-seven-point-story-structure/)
- Reader-side: flat characters with unclear motivations or inconsistent traits are a top reason readers disconnect; fix by giving characters goals and flaws — [TCK Publishing](https://www.tckpublishing.com/why-readers-drop-your-book-and-what-to-do-about-it/)
- Browne & King: introduce characters gradually ("slowly, naturally"), not all at once — [Fiction Foundry summary of Self-Editing](https://fictionfoundry.alumni.columbia.edu/seffw)
- Dialogue voice test: vary vocabulary, dialect, slang, shorthand by character; check whether each character's word choice reflects personality (careful thinker vs fast talker; speaks before thinking) — [Palomar Pressbooks, Dialogue](https://pressbooks.palomar.edu/candacerose/chapter/chapter-12-dialogue/)

### Inferences
- Protagonist audit (one line each): Want / Need / Lie / Truth / Ghost / What they do at 25% that they can't undo / What they learn at 50% / What dies at 75% / The choice at the climax that proves the arc. Any blank is a structural gap.
- Antagonist audit: Do they want the *same* object as the protagonist (Truby)? Are they as strong as the protagonist (Egri)? Do they embody the Lie or the road not taken (Story Grid)? Are they on the page at ~37% and ~62% (Weiland pinch points)?
- Supporting cast function: each should either test the theme from a different angle (Egri orchestration; B Story in Save the Cat "helps the hero change"), or apply pressure (fake-ally opponent, attack by ally in Truby). A cast member who does neither is a candidate for merging.

### Gaps
- Truby's "four-corner opposition" was not available in the fetched material.
- Found no quantitative research on "agency" tests beyond the reactive→proactive midpoint shift.

## 4. Prose and voice

### Takeaway
The line-level consensus: control psychic distance deliberately (Gardner's five-level ladder), cut filter words in close POV, show feeling through cause-and-effect rather than naming it, keep dialogue compressed with subtext and "said," and cut anything that pads, repeats or explains what the scene already showed (R.U.E.).

### Cited Findings
- Gardner's psychic distance = "the distance the reader feels between (themself) and the events in the story." Five-level ladder, far to close: (1) "It was winter of the year 1853. A large man stepped out of a doorway." (2) "Henry J Warburton had never much cared for snowstorms." (3) "Henry hated snowstorms." (4) "God how he hated these damn snowstorms." (5) "Snow. Under your collar, down inside your shoes, freezing and plugging up your miserable soul…" — [The Strangest Times, Psychic Distance](https://thestrangesttimes.substack.com/p/psychic-distance)
- Emma Darwin: distance rarely stays fixed for long, which adds dynamism; in revision, "less effective choices about distance often underlie problems that need fixing"; ask at any point how far in or out the narration is, which words create that distance, and what effect it has; prefers "Evoking" and "Informing" to show/tell — [Emma Darwin, Six Thoughts About Psychic Distance](https://emmadarwin.substack.com/p/itchy-bitesized-29-six-thoughts-about)
- Gardner on filtering: "the failure to run straight at an image; that is, the needless filtering of the image through some observing consciousness," e.g. "Turning, she noticed two snakes fighting in among the rocks." — [Leslie's Writing Exercises, Filtering](https://leslieswritingexercises.blogspot.com/2009/12/filtering.html)
- Filter word list: saw, heard, felt, knew, watched, decided, noticed, realized, wondered, thought, looked. Before/after: "Bob could see three zombies shambling toward him" → "Three zombies shambled toward Bob"; "I noticed the car was missing" → "Wait—where was her car?" Filtering is acceptable to emphasize the act of perceiving, for a dramatic scene-ending line, in far/omniscient narration, or with borderline words (wondered, realized, decided, noticed) depending on use — [Writers in the Storm, Filtering and Show Don't Tell](https://writersinthestormblog.com/2016/10/how-filtering-the-point-of-view-affects-show-dont-tell/)
- Five ways writers create unwanted narrative distance: withholding the POV character's inner thoughts; detached, unemotive narrator voice; head-hopping omniscience that never stays with one character; filter words; recounting events in retrospect rather than as they unfold — [Ladybug Panda, Increasing Narrative Distance](https://ladybug-panda.squarespace.com/blog/increasing-narrative-distance)
- Browne & King self-editing tests: (1) find sentences that state a trait/emotion directly and ask whether action could convey it; (2) scan tags for -ly adverbs and verbs other than "said"; check whether dialogue explains itself; (3) read aloud and note where your voice changes; (4) count beats around dialogue, cut if choppy; (5) interior monologue: is it explanation in disguise? remove stage directions; (6) find "as" and "-ing" constructions and rewrite if unneeded; (7) break long narration blocks with paragraphs and the occasional one-liner; (8) no POV shifts mid-scene; if needed, line space and new scene; (9) repetition: keep the stronger version, cut the weaker; (10) voice: revise weak passages toward strong ones; (11) proportion: is the rest really needed?; (12) introduce characters gradually; (13) flag "very," "rather," adjective strings, fancy imagery, italics, exclamation points; (14) condense blow-by-blow description — [Fiction Foundry summary of Self-Editing](https://fictionfoundry.alumni.columbia.edu/seffw)
- Maass's three modes of emotional writing: Inner mode (telling feelings; pitfall: stated feelings like "His guts twisted in fear" fall flat unless fresh and believable); Outer mode (implying inner state via external action; pitfall: can feel small and "written"; best as a buffer for extreme emotion: "when character emotions are highly painful, pull back"); Other mode (provoking feelings the character doesn't share, as when Montag feels excitement at a book-burning). Key principle: readers' emotional experience comes from themselves, not the character: "Action is an opportunity for us to feel something, not a cause of feeling something." — [Jane Friedman, Inner Mode Outer Mode Other Mode](https://janefriedman.com/produce-emotional-response)
- Cron: "show, don't tell" means showing the cause-and-effect chain behind a feeling, not just the feeling — [Shortform, Wired for Story](https://www.shortform.com/pdf/wired-for-story-pdf-lisa-cron)
- Dialogue: characters shouldn't state what they mean (subtext; example: a character who wants to end a relationship talks about a broken mirror); avoid on-the-nose dialogue, tension comes from what goes unsaid; don't have characters explain what the audience already knows, start the scene after the news is delivered; cut a line if the scene works without it; aim for ~three sentences/beats per speech; break exchanges with action. Tests: cut test (remove the dialogue, does the scene still work?), necessity test (does each line push story and stay true to character?), read-aloud test — [Palomar Pressbooks, Dialogue](https://pressbooks.palomar.edu/candacerose/chapter/chapter-12-dialogue/)
- Kirkus editors: stilted or excessive dialogue, excessive tagging (runs of "he said/she said" with adverbs; omit tags when the speaker is clear), telling instead of showing, bogging down in details, mixing tenses, continuity inconsistencies, passive voice — [Kirkus, Biggest Mistakes Editors See](https://kirkusreviews.com/writers-center/editing/biggest-mistakes-editors-see-and-how-avoid-them)
- King: "I believe the road to hell is paved with adverbs"; "2nd Draft = 1st Draft – 10%"; a second draft rarely fixes "incoherence or dull storytelling"; write with the door closed, rewrite with it open; cites Elmore Leonard's "leave out the boring parts" — [Signal v. Noise, Excerpts from On Writing](https://signalvnoise.com/posts/322-excerpts-from-stephen-kings-on-writing)
- Le Guin, crowding and leaping: crowding is prose "crowded with sensations, meanings and implications"; leaping is the omission that creates open space: "what you leave out is infinitely more than what you leave in"; "listing is not describing"; "Only the relevant belongs"; cut anything that "merely pads or repeats or slows or impedes your story" — [Radacts, Crowding and Leaping](https://radacts.substack.com/p/crowding-and-leaping)
- Le Guin's Steering the Craft chapter topics include the sound of language (rhythm within a sentence), the trickiness of point of view, crowding and leaping, and paragraph coherence, each with exercises — [juststartwith summary](https://juststartwith-com.pages.dev/en/creative-writing/steering-the-craft)
- Agent-side prose red flags: clichéd phrasing (hearts skipping beats, held breaths, chills down spines); purple prose (meandering, metaphor-heavy sentences) — [Renée Fountain, The Science of Rejection](https://reneefountain.substack.com/p/the-science-of-rejection-how-agents)

### Inferences
- A prose pass checklist: (1) For each scene, mark the psychic distance level at open and close and at any shift; flag shifts that jump more than two levels without a reason. (2) Regex-style search for filter words in close-POV scenes. (3) Search for -ly adverbs on dialogue tags and said-bookisms. (4) Search for "very," "rather," "suddenly," exclamation points, italics. (5) Search for "as he/she" and "-ing," constructions. (6) For each named emotion ("she felt afraid"), ask whether the cause-and-effect chain is shown. (7) For each line of dialogue, apply the cut test.
- "When telling is right": the sources support telling for (a) compression/leaping over unimportant time, (b) far psychic distance by design, (c) buffering extreme emotion (Maass outer mode), (d) summary of what a scene already dramatized elsewhere. Flag telling only when it substitutes for a scene that should have been dramatized.

### Gaps
- Did not find a primary statement of Le Guin's specific sentence-rhythm exercises or her five POV categories; only chapter topics were available.
- No source gave a quantitative "metaphor density" rule; metaphor discipline is covered only as the "purple prose" and "fancy imagery" warnings.

## 5. Pacing and tension

### Takeaway
Tension is required at two scales: Maass's line-level micro-tension (so readers cannot skim) and the macro escalation of stakes through progressive complications, try/fail cycles and pinch points; the "sagging middle" is fixed by giving Act 2 its own beats (pinches, midpoint reversal) rather than filler.

### Cited Findings
- Maass: micro-tension is "the line-by-line tension that authors create in the mind of the reader"; readers want to relieve unease, and the only way is to keep reading; constant micro-tension makes skimming hard, which is what makes a page-turner. "Most writers don't have enough"; "What's too much tension for you to bear is probably just enough to keep your reader engaged." He rejects the idea that tension must be paused for pacing — [Mythic Scribes interview with Donald Maass](https://mythicscribes.com/interviews/donald-maass/)
- Maass's exercise ("tension on every page"): pick any paragraph and sentence and ask how to raise its tension; strengthen verbs, nouns and metaphors so they resonate emotionally; keep adding — [Janet Fox, What Is Micro-Tension](https://janetfox.substack.com/p/what-is-micro-tension)
- Progressive complications must escalate; the same level shouldn't repeat or the story becomes boring — [Story Grid, 5 Commandments Revisited](https://storygrid.com/5-commandments-storytelling-revisited/)
- Sagging middle causes: treating the middle as filler/delay; missing obstacles; no failed attempts; unplanned stretches (pantsers run out around 20-30K words); impatience to reach the climax. Fixes: add pinch points at the 3/8 and 5/8 marks that reveal the antagonist or raise stakes; use the midpoint as a reflection beat where the protagonist reassesses what success means; keep a vague outline; show the potential the character could lose; write in order — [Jami Gold, How to Avoid a Sagging Middle](https://jamigold.com/2013/10/how-to-avoid-a-sagging-middle-in-our-stories/)
- Dramatic irony = the audience knows something the characters don't; Hitchcock's bomb: surprise (nobody knows, audience startled for seconds) vs suspense (audience knows, characters don't, anticipation drives tension). Fiction examples: Munro's letters written by someone other than the supposed author; Meehan's opening line announcing the protagonist's death; Kalfus's plutonium strapped to a chest; Thomas's narrator whose blind spots the reader sees — [Electric Literature, How to Use Dramatic Irony](https://electricliterature.com/how-to-use-dramatic-irony-read-like-a-writer/)
- Cron: withhold information but hint in advance so readers sense something is missing; foreshadow any out-of-character choice — [Shortform, Wired for Story](https://www.shortform.com/pdf/wired-for-story-pdf-lisa-cron)
- Chapter endings: "Tension comes from inside the POV character's emotional reactions to the action." A novella ending on an unresolved phone call made ~20% of reviewers feel cheated and stopped the author buying the sequel — [Pikes Peak Writers, Tension and Cliffhangers](https://pikespeakwriters.org/tension-and-cliffhangers/)
- Scene length variation: commercial scenes 1,000-5,000 words, ~2,000 typical — [Story Grid, How Many Scenes](https://storygrid.com/414/); Ingermanson scenes 1-12 pages, average 4 — [Advanced Fiction Writing](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/blog/2009/05/25/scenes-sequels-and-chapters/)
- Environmental friction (a stuck latch, a closing crowd) creates tension without stopping the plot — [Not MFA, Setting That Drives Story](https://notmfa.substack.com/p/how-to-write-setting-that-drives)
- Reader data: "slow and boring" was the top reason for abandoning a book at 46.4% of respondents in a Goodreads survey; reasons #2 and #3 also related to slowness/boredom — [Tyson Adams, Why Are Books Abandoned](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/4505032-why-are-books-abandoned)
- Reader-side pacing complaints: scenes that drag *and* important moments rushed past both lose readers; fix by alternating intense and quiet scenes and cutting description/dialogue that doesn't advance story — [TCK Publishing](https://www.tckpublishing.com/why-readers-drop-your-book-and-what-to-do-about-it/)

### Inferences
- Macro-tension audit: list each major obstacle in order and verify each is harder than the last (no repeated difficulty level); list try/fail outcomes and verify a mix of "yes, but" and "no, and" rather than repeated clean successes.
- Information-management audit: for each secret or reveal, decide whether the reader should know before the character (suspense/dramatic irony) or with them (surprise), and confirm the choice is deliberate; verify every reveal was foreshadowed.
- Chapter-ending audit: ending should be a question, decision, or emotional turn tied to the POV character's stakes, not a withheld-outcome gimmick; reserve true cliffhangers.

### Gaps
- The Goodreads survey's #2 and #3 reasons were not given with percentages in the available source.
- Maass's "public vs personal stakes" and "conflicting emotions" techniques were not available in fetched material (the interview did not cover them).

## 6. Theme and meaning

### Takeaway
Egri's premise, McKee's controlling idea and Story Grid's controlling idea are the same tool: a single cause-and-effect sentence that states what value wins and why, proven by the climax; preachiness is avoided by letting the counter-idea win scenes and by embodying the argument in the protagonist's internal struggle rather than in speeches.

### Cited Findings
- Egri's premise implies character, conflict and conclusion. "Great love defies even death": "great love" = character, "defies" = conflict, "even death" = ending. Rising conflict is "the natural result of a clear premise and well-orchestrated characters" — [SuperSummary, Art of Dramatic Writing](https://www.supersummary.com/the-art-of-dramatic-writing/summary)
- McKee's controlling idea: "a specific, truthful statement about the world that expresses cause and effect," usually pairing the story's final value change with the reason for it, e.g. Finding Nemo: "Children live fulfilling lives when parents allow them to take risks." Counter-idea/anti-theme: each scene's climactic value change proves either the theme or its opposite; the story alternates between them until the climax, where the theme wins. Contrasted with one-word themes like "family," which produce related-looking scenes with no shared meaning. The alternation conveys the message "without ever explicitly telling it to the audience" — [Shortform, What Is a Controlling Idea](https://www.shortform.com/blog/what-is-a-controlling-idea/)
- Story Grid lists Controlling Idea/Theme as one of the Editor's Six Core Questions, and classifies stories as Prescriptive (rewards the right choice) or Cautionary (punishes the wrong one; Gatsby, Gone Girl) — [Story Grid Six Core Questions](https://storygrid.com/editors-six-core-questions-part-1/); [Story Grid, Protagonist and Antagonist](https://storygrid.com/protagonist-and-antagonist/)
- Save the Cat places "Theme Stated" at ~5%, usually spoken by a secondary character, and the B Story carries the thematic/relational thread — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/save-the-cat-beat-sheet/)
- Signs of a preachy theme: heavy-handed delivery with plot/characters as soapbox; one-sided exploration (opposing views weak or villainized); no inner struggle (goals met too easily); characters discuss the theme at length while events do little; polarized readers. Three fixes: show both sides through conflict (give antagonists believable motivation and a voice); develop internal conflict first and draw the theme from it; show the theme in action through events, choices and recurring symbols, with dialogue alongside rather than instead — [DIY MFA, Revising Preachy Themes](https://diymfa.com/writing/revising-preachy-themes)
- Five tips: clarity and consistency (theme steady start to finish); avoid preaching, let readers discover it (don't make characters mouthpieces); balance theme and story; avoid cliché and one-dimensional idea-characters; "resonance, not resolution" (some threads may stay open). "A story is never made better by sermonizing." — [Live Write Thrive, Theme in Fiction](https://www.livewritethrive.com/2025/08/04/theme-in-fiction-5-tips-and-pitfalls/)
- Weiland: the Lie/Truth pair *is* the thematic argument; the Third Plot Point is where "a cherished lie dies" — [Janet's Writing Blog on Weiland](https://janetswritingblog.com/2022/01/17/trying-to-get-novel-structure-right/); [Shortform, Creating Character Arcs](https://www.shortform.com/pdf/creating-character-arcs-pdf-k-m-weiland)

### Inferences
- Theme checklist: (1) Write the controlling idea as "[Value] prevails/fails when/because [cause]." (2) Write the counter-idea. (3) Tag every scene's value shift as proving idea or counter-idea; if the counter-idea never wins a scene, the book is one-sided. (4) Confirm the protagonist's Lie = the counter-idea and Truth = the controlling idea, so the arc embodies the argument. (5) Search dialogue for lines that state the theme outright; keep at most one, early (Theme Stated), ideally from someone the protagonist disagrees with. (6) Confirm the antagonist can articulate their side convincingly.

### Gaps
- Did not retrieve Story Grid's own formal definition of controlling idea beyond its listing as a core question.

## 7. Setting and worldbuilding

### Takeaway
Setting earns its place when it applies pressure, is filtered through the POV character's desire or fear, and is rendered in a few selected sensory details; Hemingway's iceberg principle governs how much of the author's knowledge should surface.

### Cited Findings
- Hemingway (Death in the Afternoon): "The dignity of movement of an ice-berg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water." A writer who knows the subject can omit things and the reader will still feel them; omitting from ignorance creates "hollow places." Examples: "Hills Like White Elephants" never names the procedure; "Big Two-Hearted River" never mentions the war — [Wikipedia, Iceberg theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iceberg_theory)
- Setting is pressure, not backdrop: rooms limit choices, weather heightens conflict, architecture controls pacing; "if nothing in the environment forces a decision, the setting is just a painted backdrop." Setting is experienced, not inventoried (worldbuilding is reference material; setting is how the world feels inside a scene). Filter every detail through the POV character's desire, fear or bias; "if a sentence could appear unchanged in another character's voice, it isn't doing POV work." Use three to five specific sensory details that match the scene's emotional logic, drawing on temperature, smell, texture and sound, not just sight. Use blocking (moving, touching, colliding) to keep space active. Let recurring images evolve as the character changes. Tests: Blindfold test (could a reader understand the space's pressures without seeing it?); Filter test (can each detail be justified by the POV character's current desire or fear?) — [Not MFA, How to Write Setting That Drives Story](https://notmfa.substack.com/p/how-to-write-setting-that-drives)
- Le Guin: "listing is not describing"; "Only the relevant belongs" — [Radacts, Crowding and Leaping](https://radacts.substack.com/p/crowding-and-leaping)
- Agents: info-dumping (long backstory or worldbuilding up front) slows pace and delays the main action; overwritten openings make the manuscript "a heavy lift" — [Renée Fountain](https://reneefountain.substack.com/p/the-science-of-rejection-how-agents)
- Editors: "getting bogged down in details"; keep a clear mental picture of the world but include only the details that matter — [Kirkus](https://kirkusreviews.com/writers-center/editing/biggest-mistakes-editors-see-and-how-avoid-them)
- Browne & King: break up long blocks of description; condense blow-by-blow; introduce exposition gradually — [Fiction Foundry summary](https://fictionfoundry.alumni.columbia.edu/seffw)
- Cron: readers hunt for why each piece of information matters; random events and unnecessary flashbacks fragment the story; flashbacks only when essential — [Shortform, Wired for Story](https://www.shortform.com/pdf/wired-for-story-pdf-lisa-cron)

### Inferences
- Exposition rule derived from the sources: any paragraph of world information must pass one of three gates: it changes a decision in this scene (pressure), it is something the POV character would notice right now (filter), or the reader needs it to understand the next action (relevance). Otherwise move it later or cut it (iceberg).
- Infodump detector: a block of three or more consecutive sentences of narration with no character action, no sensory anchor, and no present-tense consequence.

### Gaps
- Found no editor survey quantifying how much description is "too much"; all guidance is qualitative.

## 8. Openings and endings

### Takeaway
Agents and editors decide within the first five to ten pages and look for a clear protagonist, something at stake, a point of change, and a distinct voice; satisfying endings are surprising yet inevitable, answer the question the opening raised, follow an unbroken causal and emotional chain, and show the character changed.

### Cited Findings

**Openings**
- Agents often decide within "the first five to 10 pages," sometimes within a few paragraphs. Criteria: does the opening make the reader care what happens next; is it submission-ready; is the voice fresh; do the first pages introduce compelling characters, hint at conflict, set tone and stakes. Rejection triggers: info-dumping; overwritten openings; clichéd scenes (waking up, mirror self-description, dream sequences, weather small talk); clichéd phrasing; overused premises (chosen one; romance built on a hidden secret); purple prose; derivative work; slow starts (if the hook arrives late, cut earlier material or hint conflict sooner); taste/list fit — [Renée Fountain, The Science of Rejection](https://reneefountain.substack.com/p/the-science-of-rejection-how-agents)
- Jane Friedman / Alice Pope first-page critique of 25 submissions: begin at a point of change, not before; protagonist clear and early; something must happen (action, problem, interaction); distinct voice and unique angle; the reader must be able to follow what's happening. Problems cited: opening with flashback; starting at the wrong point; slowing with backstory; alarm clock / ringing phone / getting out of bed; unclear protagonist; too many characters at once; no action or conflict; ordinary crisis without voice or twist; heavy telling; mostly internal thought with no interaction; journal/stream-of-consciousness feel; confusing events with too little context — [Writer's Digest, The First Five Minutes](https://www.writersdigest.com/getting-published/the-first-five-minutes-how-editors-evaluate-your-manuscript)
- Cron: on page one readers look for "Who's the story about? What's the story about? What's at stake here?" Without these, strong prose won't hold attention — [Shortform, Wired for Story](https://www.shortform.com/pdf/wired-for-story-pdf-lisa-cron)
- Developmental editor Nicole Meier: "too much gearing up in the beginning"; fix: "Drop readers into an active scene"; "Start late and end early!" — [Nicole Meier, 10 Mistakes I See in Manuscripts](https://nicolemeierwrites.substack.com/p/10-mistakes-i-see-in-manuscripts) (paywalled after item 1)
- Save the Cat: Opening Image at 0-1% sets tone and is mirrored by the Final Image; Catalyst by ~10% — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/save-the-cat-beat-sheet/)
- Reader-side: readers often decide within the first few pages; fix: open with a question, mystery or conflict — [TCK Publishing](https://www.tckpublishing.com/why-readers-drop-your-book-and-what-to-do-about-it/)

**Endings**
- Surprising yet inevitable: only surprising feels like a cheat, only inevitable feels predictable. The ending should answer the questions the opening raises; ambiguous endings can satisfy, overly tidy ones ring false. Tests: causal chain (each action follows the last via "because"); emotional logic (feelings follow from events); meaning from change (characters end different from who they were). "The material is in the material." — [Writers.com, Better Endings](https://writers.com/?p=19837)
- Story Grid Ending Payoff: fulfills the hook's promises in unexpected ways, surprising yet inevitable, answers the central question, restores order — [Story Grid, Hook Build Payoff](https://storygrid.com/hook-build-payoff/)
- Seven-Point: Resolution shows how the character changed from the Hook state (the opposite) — [Writing Excuses 7.41](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-7-41-seven-point-story-structure/)
- Final Image mirrors or contrasts the Opening Image (99-100%) — [Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/save-the-cat-beat-sheet/)
- Weiland: Climax ~88% where protagonist confronts antagonist and the goal's outcome is decided; Resolution ties loose ends — [Janet's Writing Blog](https://janetswritingblog.com/2022/01/17/trying-to-get-novel-structure-right/)
- Theme: "resonance, not resolution" — some thematic threads may stay open — [Live Write Thrive](https://www.livewritethrive.com/2025/08/04/theme-in-fiction-5-tips-and-pitfalls/)
- Coyne: an irreversible character change by the end of the Ending Payoff — [Story Grid 5 Commandments](https://storygrid.com/5-commandments-storytelling-revisited/)

### Inferences
- First-pages checklist (merged agent/editor criteria): by page 1-2, a named POV character doing something with a discernible want; by page 5, a sense of what could go wrong (stakes) and a tonal/voice signature; by ~10%, the catalyst; no alarm clocks, dreams, mirrors, weather, prologue infodumps, or more than ~3 named characters before the protagonist is anchored.
- Ending checklist: (1) Name the story question posed in Act 1 and the sentence where it is answered. (2) Does the climax require the protagonist to act on the Truth learned (not luck, not a new character)? (3) Trace the "because" chain from Third Plot Point to climax with no gaps. (4) Does the final image answer the opening image? (5) Were all setups paid off and all payoffs set up (Sanderson's promise/progress/payoff)? (6) Is the denouement proportionate (short) and does at least one thread deliberately remain resonant rather than tidy?

### Gaps
- No source gave a percentage or page-count norm for denouement length.

## 9. Revision and editing

### Takeaway
Editors agree on the order: rest, read-through, structural, character, scene, line, copy, proofread; fixing sentences before structure wastes work because large changes delete polished prose.

### Cited Findings
- Levels: Developmental (structure, pacing, character development, what to add/remove/reorganize); Line (voice, word choice, rhythm, tone consistency; doesn't restructure); Copy (grammar, spelling, punctuation, usage, internal consistency, style guide: "asks not whether the writing is effective, but whether it's correct"); Proofreading (surface errors and formatting after editing). Order: developmental → line → copy → proofread; "Skipping ahead or going out of sequence wastes investment" — [Editor World, Types of Editing](https://www.editorworld.com/article/types-of-editing)
- Multi-pass self-revision: rest the draft for weeks; (1) Read-through without editing, noting where attention drops or confusion arises; (2) Structural: does each major event earn its place; cut, reorder, rewrite; (3) Character: follow each major character through; protagonist arc builds and pays off; antagonist applies real pressure; motivations consistent; (4) Scene: each scene turns; dialogue does more than deliver information; (5) Line: rhythm, word choice, filler, images; (6) Proofread. Reasoning: problems exist at different scales; large changes alter smaller work; one focus per pass — [Editor World, How to Revise a Novel](https://www.editorworld.com/article/how-to-revise-a-novel)
- Most-cited manuscript problems (Kirkus editors): losing focus (confirm there is a hook and a climax); inconsistent tone; stilted/excessive dialogue; excessive tagging; telling instead of showing; bogged down in details; mixing tenses; inconsistency in details; passive voice — [Kirkus](https://kirkusreviews.com/writers-center/editing/biggest-mistakes-editors-see-and-how-avoid-them)
- Most-cited first-page problems (Friedman/Pope): see Section 8 — [Writer's Digest](https://www.writersdigest.com/getting-published/the-first-five-minutes-how-editors-evaluate-your-manuscript)
- Browne & King's 14 self-editing checks (see Section 4) — [Fiction Foundry](https://fictionfoundry.alumni.columbia.edu/seffw)
- King: second draft = first draft minus 10%; incoherence or dull storytelling can't be fixed by a second draft (i.e. needs structural work); write privately, rewrite publicly — [Signal v. Noise](https://signalvnoise.com/posts/322-excerpts-from-stephen-kings-on-writing)
- Le Guin: in revision cut anything that "merely pads or repeats or slows or impedes your story" — [Radacts](https://radacts.substack.com/p/crowding-and-leaping)
- Reader-side: typos and formatting errors signal lack of professionalism and lose readers; use beta readers and a developmental editor; use KDP page-read data to find where readers stop — [TCK Publishing](https://www.tckpublishing.com/why-readers-drop-your-book-and-what-to-do-about-it/)
- Story Grid spreadsheet method: log every scene with its value shift, polarity, turning point, POV, word count — [Story Grid, How to Spreadsheet Your Novel](https://storygrid.com/how-to-spreadsheet-your-novel/) (from search result title; not fetched)

### Inferences
- A reviewer "skill" architecture following the editing levels: Structural reviewer (Section 1 + 8 checks) → Character reviewer (Section 3) → Scene reviewer (Section 2) → Theme reviewer (Section 6) → Line reviewer (Section 4 + 7) → Copy reviewer. Each should refuse to report lower-level findings until higher-level ones are resolved, matching the editors' "don't polish what might be cut" rule.

### Gaps
- No single survey ranking developmental problems by frequency was found; the Kirkus and Friedman lists are editor opinion, not quantified data.

## 10. Reader expectations

### Takeaway
The only quantitative reader data found says boredom/slowness dominates abandonment (46.4% in a Goodreads survey); editor and craft commentary converges on immersion (clear POV, no confusion), promise-and-payoff (genre/marketing match, setups paid off), emotional payoff (readers feel their own emotions, not the character's), and clarity.

### Cited Findings
- Goodreads survey: "slow and boring" cited by 46.4% as the top reason to abandon; #2 and #3 reasons also relate to slowness/boredom; 36.6% keep reading mainly because they like to finish what they start — [Tyson Adams, Why Are Books Abandoned](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/4505032-why-are-books-abandoned)
- Reasons readers drop books (editor commentary): slow or confusing openings; flat characters with unclear motivations; pacing (dragging or rushed); unmet expectations when cover/blurb promise a different story; weak dialogue (unrealistic, info-dumping, on-the-nose, missing tags); editing/formatting errors; predictability or excessive complexity (too many characters, convoluted plots) — [TCK Publishing](https://www.tckpublishing.com/why-readers-drop-your-book-and-what-to-do-about-it/)
- Story Grid: genre is "the promise made to the audience"; conventions and obligatory scenes are what audiences expect; unexplained POV shifts signal weak craft — [Story Grid Six Core Questions](https://storygrid.com/editors-six-core-questions-part-1/)
- Sanderson: promise, progress, payoff is central to plotting; stakes must build gradually — [Sanderson Lecture 3](https://brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-2025-overview-of-story-structure-lecture-3)
- Maass: readers' emotional experience comes mainly from themselves; "Action is an opportunity for us to feel something, not a cause of feeling something"; readers keep reading to relieve micro-tension — [Jane Friedman, Maass modes](https://janefriedman.com/produce-emotional-response); [Mythic Scribes](https://mythicscribes.com/interviews/donald-maass/)
- Cron: readers hunt for meaning and want to know why each piece of information matters; story is a mental simulation; everything must be relevant to the story question — [Shortform, Wired for Story](https://www.shortform.com/pdf/wired-for-story-pdf-lisa-cron)
- Kishōtenketsu readers' payoff comes from understanding rather than winning — [StorytellingDB](https://storytellingdb.com/kishotenketsu/)
- Cliffhanger backlash: ~20% of reviewers felt cheated by an unresolved ending — [Pikes Peak Writers](https://pikespeakwriters.org/tension-and-cliffhangers/)

### Inferences
- Reader-expectation checklist: (1) Does the book deliver the genre's obligatory scenes? (2) Is every setup paid off and every payoff set up? (3) Is there never a stretch of more than one scene where nothing is at stake for the POV character? (4) Can a reader always answer "whose scene is this and what do they want?" (5) Does the ending resolve the central question even if subplots stay open?

### Gaps
- The StorytellingDB claim that "brain scans show character-driven stories activate empathy centers" cites no source; treat as unverified.
- No large-scale, recent (post-2020) quantitative reader-abandonment study was found; the Goodreads survey is the only one with numbers and its date was not visible in the fetched page.
